Every claim carries a number from the run log. R-hat, divergences, a predictive p-value, an elpd difference with its standard error, an interval on the decision.

Full marks, one example each. Model: pooling defended by who it moves. Computation: diagnostics with numbers and a calibration that passes. Checking: a statistic chosen for the decision and a comparison with the standard error of the difference. Decision: expected utility, remaining risk, the value of one piece of data. Reproducibility: a fresh clone reruns to the same numbers. The rubric is 60% of the grade; the supervised check and the oral 20% each.

Format

8 minutes to present, 7 to defend

1 · Brief and model

The decision maker's question; the structure and the priors you would defend.

2 · Evidence

Diagnostics, the check that mattered, the comparison of models.

3 · Decision

The choice, its interval, the remaining risk, what more data is worth.

The questions. The assessor changes one prior or one cost; you predict the effect on the decision, then rerun. Then your peer asks the question from their review of your model.

The prediction comes before the rerun and is scored on its reasoning, not on being exactly right. Typical changes: a wider prior on the spread, a cost per finding doubled, a budget cut by a third. Write the change and the prediction in your scoring notes: they are the authenticity evidence the catalog asks for.

What a strong defence sounds like

Predict in words first, then check with the model

Strong

"Doubling the value of a serious finding raises the stakes in schools with one visit; I expect three or four of them to enter the 30 visits, at the cost of large schools with known moderate rates." Then the rerun.

Weak

"The results should be robust." Or: rerunning before saying anything, then describing what changed.

Share this before the first oral so nobody is surprised by the format. The example is for the school inspections brief; adapt it to the brief of the participant in front of you.

After the course

The follow-up task, within three weeks

  • Review one real uncertainty analysis from your workplace or a public report, with the checklist of Lab 4.4.
  • Submit at most 800 words: the findings, quoting the analysis, ranked by their effect on the decision, and a three-sentence verdict. labs/followup/README.md has the details.
  • Keep your capstone repository reproducible: it is the evidence of the course's outcomes.

The follow-up is tracked: the Academy contacts each participant at three weeks. An analysis from work may contain restricted data: participants submit their findings, not the analysis, and follow their organisation's data classification.
