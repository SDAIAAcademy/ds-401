The question LO2 asks

Not "is pooling allowed?" but "what does this pooling do to which districts, and can you defend it?"

A pointer, not a lesson: DS-301 Day 4 session 2 is the frequentist version. Participants who skipped it can still follow; the three poolings are defined on the next slide.

Three ways to treat 140 districts

No pooling trusts each district; complete pooling trusts none

No pooling

One rate per district, each from its own record. Tiny districts get wild estimates; 13 with no recorded burst look perfectly safe.

Complete pooling

One rate for all (Lab 1.3). Every district is average, including the old asbestos-cement ones.

Partial pooling

District rates drawn from a common distribution with spread τ. The data decide how much each district borrows.

Ask the room which of the three the consultant on Day 4 used (no pooling). Partial pooling is the only one where how much a district is pulled is estimated rather than decided by the analyst.

Lab 2.1, task 1

One line makes the districts a population

alpha = numpyro.sample("alpha", dist.Normal(np.log(0.10), 1.0))
tau = numpyro.sample("tau", dist.HalfNormal(0.5))
with numpyro.plate("district", J):
    eta = numpyro.sample("eta", dist.Normal(alpha, tau))   # the population
numpyro.sample("y", dist.Poisson(jnp.exp(log_km + eta[d])), obs=y)

τ near 0 is complete pooling; τ very large is no pooling. The posterior of τ chooses between them.

The prior on τ, HalfNormal(0.5), puts 95% of its weight on τ below about 1: the typical district (one standard deviation) rarely sits more than a factor of e (2.7) away from what the mean predicts. Lab 3.4 tests how much that choice matters. This is the centred form; it samples well on all 140 districts, and session 3 shows where it does not.

LO2's evidence

A defence of pooling says what it does to whom

  1. Why partial pooling: 51 districts recorded fewer than five bursts; alone, their estimates are noise.
  2. What it does: small districts borrow from the population, here up to half their estimate.
  3. Whom it affects: the rural districts, whose risk it understates while one urban-dominated rate is the target.
  4. What changes next: shrink towards a prediction for each district's material and age.

Lab 2.1 task 5 asks for three sentences; this slide is a fuller version for the capstone README. A defence that only says "partial pooling is the standard choice" earns nothing on the rubric's model criterion.

Group-level predictors

Shrink each district towards what its pipes predict, not towards the average

mu = alpha + X @ beta          # material and age
eta = mu + tau * z             # district effect around it
  • A rural asbestos-cement district of 40 years is now pulled towards the rate of such networks.
  • τ now measures what material and age leave unexplained.
  • The district-level predictors cost no data from the small districts: they are known for all 140.

The code is written in non-centred form, as the lab asks; session 3 explains why. X has four columns: age in decades above 30, and indicators for ductile iron, cast iron and asbestos cement, PVC being the base.

Why fit B diverged

When τ is small, every district effect must squeeze into a narrow neck

Sketch. In Lab 2.3's run, every one of the centred model's 59 divergences had τ below its median.

The step size tuned in warm-up suits the wide mouth, where most of the volume is; in the neck it is far too large, the path's energy blows up, and the sampler reports a divergence and never explores the neck. Small τ is exactly the region that says "these districts are alike": missing it overstates the spread.

Sources: Betancourt and Girolami (2013), Hamiltonian Monte Carlo for Hierarchical Models.

Same model, different coordinates

Non-centred: sample standard district effects, then scale them by τ

eta = sample("eta", Normal(mu, tau))
z = sample("z", Normal(0, 1))
eta = deterministic("eta", mu + tau * z)

The joint distribution is identical. In z, the posterior has no funnel: its shape no longer depends on τ.

Stan's User's Guide calls this the non-centred parameterisation; NumPyro also offers it as a reparameterisation handler (LocScaleReparam), which participants may meet in the documentation. The lab asks them to write it by hand so they see it is three lines.

Not a rule of thumb

With strong data in every group, the centred form can be the better one

All 140 districts, centred

No divergence, bulk ESS above 1,000 in Lab 2.1: large districts pin their own effects, so there is no funnel.

49 small districts, centred

59 divergences: little data per district, the prior's funnel dominates.

Choose the form by the data, then check. The diagnostics, not a habit, decide.

When every group has a lot of data, the non-centred form can show the opposite geometry and sample worse. The course defaults to non-centred because Yanabi's districts are mostly thin, and checks every fit.

Thinning a Poisson count keeps it Poisson with the mean multiplied by p; the same holds for the negative binomial of Day 3. Districts with sensors have p = 1 by assumption: an assumption the memo states.

An informative prior, with its source

The 2024 audit sets the prior the data cannot replace

  • Ouda's team listened to 6 districts without sensors for a season: 28 of 40 bursts found had been reported.
  • Beta(14, 6): mean 0.70, 90% interval 0.52 to 0.85; the audit at half weight.
  • Half weight because six districts in one season may not represent all 76.

The reason for this prior

Cites a measurement, its size and why it was discounted: the standard of Day 1, applied to an informative prior.

Beta(28, 12), the audit at full weight, gives 0.58 to 0.81. The choice between them is a judgement to state, not to hide. The audit itself is part of the synthetic case.
