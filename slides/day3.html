What a pooled estimate rests on

A rural district of 1.5 km recorded one burst in five years. Its posterior rate under the Lab 2.4 model is 0.21. What contributes most to that number?

Its own record: 1 burst over 7.5 km-years
The rate predicted for its material and age, plus the audit prior on reporting
The sampler's starting values
The common rate of all 140 districts
Why. With so little data the district borrows almost everything: from the prediction for its pipes (Lab 2.2) and, without sensors, from the reporting prior (Lab 2.4).

Answer: B. Its own record alone would say 0.13, which is noise at this size. The question sets up today: if the estimate rests on the model, the model has to be checked.

Lab 3.1, task 3

A negative binomial lets each year vary more than a Poisson

phi = numpyro.sample("phi", dist.Gamma(2.0, 0.2))
numpyro.sample("y", dist.NegativeBinomial2(mean, phi), obs=y)
# variance = mean + mean**2 / phi
  • φ = 7.0, 90% interval 5.6 to 9.0 (the generating value was 6).
  • Both statistics now sit inside the replicates: p = 0.34 and 0.48.
  • Weather, pressure surges and repair campaigns move a district's year; the Poisson has no room for them.

The Gamma(2, 0.2) prior has mean 10: mild extra variation expected, large values (close to Poisson) allowed. A participant who writes NegativeBinomial with (total_count, probs) instead of NegativeBinomial2 gets a different parameterisation: the lab's check catches the wrong mean.

Lab 3.1, stretch

A histogram of all district-years cannot tell the two models apart

Square root of the frequencies, from the course's quick run. The dispersion within each district, 2.32 against Poisson replicates of 0.87 to 1.13, separates the same two models at once.

Ask the room to predict the stretch's picture before showing it: most expect the Poisson to miss. It does not, because the district effects already spread the counts across districts; the extra variation is within each district from year to year, and a histogram of all 700 district-years mixes it with the differences between districts. The lesson is the session's: a check exposes a misfit only if its statistic looks where the misfit is. The quiet years tell the same story (p = 0.12): district effects already give small districts their zeros.

Comparing models by predictive accuracy

  • Compare models by the difference and its standard error, not by the ranking alone

02
Comparing models by predictive accuracy

Score each model on the district-years it did not see

  • elpd: the expected log predictive density of a new district-year; higher is better.
  • Leave-one-out refits 700 times; PSIS-LOO approximates all of them from one fit by importance sampling.
  • Pareto k says, for each point, whether the approximation can be trusted (below about 0.7).

Why not R² or the fit to the data

Any model with more parameters fits the data it saw better. Predicting what it did not see is the fair contest.

The consultant's report on Day 4 offers R² of observed against fitted as evidence of fit; this slide is the answer to it. p_loo, the effective number of parameters, is a useful aside: the hierarchical Poisson spends 158 of them because it explains extra variation with district effects.

Source: Vehtari, Gelman and Gabry (2017), Practical Bayesian model evaluation using leave-one-out cross-validation and WAIC.

Session 3

Is the code right

  • Recover known parameters from data simulated by the model
  • Run a short simulation-based calibration and read its rank histograms
  • Locate and fix an implementation error from the parameter that fails
03
Is the code right

One successful recovery proves little

  • A colleague simulated one dataset and recovered both parameters: α and β inside their 90% intervals.
  • The model still has an error. One run passes by luck as easily as by being right.
  • A correct model misses the truth 10% of the time; a wrong one can cover it often.

Lab 3.3's starting point

20 northern districts, 445 km, a Poisson rate with an age effect. The simulator is right; one line of the model is not.

Do not reveal the line. Ask the room how they test their own model code today; most will say "it ran and the numbers looked sensible". That is what the colleague did.

Simulation-based calibration

If the code is right, the truth's rank among the draws is uniform

1 · Simulateparameters from the prior, then data
→
2 · Fitthe model to those data: 99 draws
→
3 · Rankcount draws below the true value: 0 to 99
→
4 · Repeat100 times; plot each parameter's ranks
Shapes to read. Flat: right. U: posterior too narrow. Hump: too wide. Slope: biased.

The argument in one sentence: averaged over the prior, the posterior is the prior, so a true value drawn from the prior is just another draw from the posterior and its rank is uniform. Any departure is a mismatch between the simulator and the fitted model, in code or in maths.

Source: Talts, Betancourt, Simpson, Vehtari and Gelman (2018), Validating Bayesian Inference Algorithms with Simulation-Based Calibration.

Quick run, 100 simulations. α is pulled a little too (33%, not significant at 100 simulations) because a wrong β pushes the intercept to compensate: look first at the parameter with the strongest signal. NumPyro, like Stan and SciPy, takes a standard deviation; some textbooks write the normal with a variance. This is one of the most common real errors in hand-written models.

What calibration does not do

Calibration tests the code against the simulator, not the model against the world

It finds

Coding errors, wrong parameterisations, a sampler that cannot fit the model.

It misses

The same mistake in both the simulator and the model; a model that is wrong about Yanabi.

It costs

100 fits per calibration. Small models and short chains make it a few minutes on a laptop.

Session 1's checks test the model against the data; this session tests the code against its own assumptions. The capstone asks for both. The stretch item calibrates a derived quantity, the rate at age 40, which participants report to Nouran.

Checkpoint

Reading rank histograms

In a calibration of 200 simulations, a parameter's ranks form a hump: few near 0 or 99, many in the middle. What does it say?

The posterior is approximately normal, as expected
The posterior is too narrow
The posterior is too wide: the truth sits near its middle too often
The sampler needs more warm-up
Why. A right posterior puts the truth anywhere among its draws equally often; a too-wide one rarely puts it in the tails.

Answer: C. A typical cause is a prior in the model wider than the simulator's, the mirror image of today's error.

The summary for Nouran

The prior that matters is the one on reporting, and it rests on one audit

  1. The priors on spread and shape barely move the decision quantities.
  2. The reporting prior moves the rural no-sensor rates almost one for one (Lab 2.4's stretch).
  3. So the money for better information goes to reporting, not to refining the model: Day 4, session 3.

This is the three-sentence summary Lab 3.4 asks for, written for a director. A summary that says "the model is robust to the priors" without naming the one prior that matters fails the LO3 standard.

Day 3 · wrap-up

Three things to keep

Check what the decision needs

Choose statistics the fit does not force; the variation from year to year sank the Poisson.

Test the code, not one run

Calibration over many simulations found a line that one recovery missed.

Name the prior that matters

Spread and shape priors barely move the plan; the reporting prior does.

Ask each table for the one row of today's comparison table that would change Nouran's decision if it had gone the other way. Usually: the dispersion check, because it widens every district's interval.
