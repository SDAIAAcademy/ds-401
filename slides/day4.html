01
Loss, utility and the decision table

Three actions, one cost each, computed for every posterior draw

cost(action) = cost_per_km * km + rate * km * 80_000 * (20 - years * reduction)
ActionSAR per kmFewer burstsFor
Wait00%–
Repair weak joints, manage pressure40,00030%10 years
Re-pipe the mains400,00090%20 years

Synthetic planning numbers (data/costs.json); a burst costs SAR 80,000; horizon 20 years.

Work one district on the board: 10 km at 0.25 bursts per km-year expects 50 bursts in 20 years. Wait: SAR 4.0 million. Repair: 0.4 million plus 42.5 bursts, 3.8 million. Re-pipe: 4.0 million plus 5 bursts, 4.4 million. Repair wins at this rate. Per kilometre, waiting is cheapest below about 0.17 bursts per km-year and re-piping above 0.30. Expected utility does this for every draw, then averages.

Step by step

Average the cost over the posterior, then choose

1 · Draws2,000 posterior rates for each of 140 districts
→
2 · Costseach action's cost in each draw: 3 × 2,000 × 140
→
3 · Choosethe action with the lowest expected cost in each district
Result. Wait in 87 districts, repair 38, re-pipe 15.

Numbers from data/posterior/rates.npz, the posterior of the model chosen on Day 3. Order matters: average the cost, then choose; choosing per draw and taking the most frequent action is a different and worse rule.

Deciding on the median rate gets three rural districts wrong

The mean plug-in agrees everywhere: these costs are straight lines in the rate. The median sits below the mean of a skewed posterior.

Be honest about the size: with linear costs, deciding on the posterior mean is exactly right, so the Bayesian advantage today is not in this table. It is in the budget, where the interval of the plan matters, and in the survey, where the whole distribution decides the value. A report of medians is the common case, which is why these three districts are worth showing. The stretch's cautious rule (lowest 90th percentile of cost) changes 43 districts and costs SAR 11.6 million more on average: the price of caution, to be chosen knowingly.

Checkpoint

Expected cost or cost at the estimate

An extra cost applies only when a district exceeds 40 bursts in a year: a fine from the regulator. Does deciding on the posterior mean rate still give the expected-utility choice?

Yes: the mean plug-in is always the same as expected utility
Not in general: the fine is not linear in the rate, so its expectation depends on the whole posterior
No, but the median plug-in would
No decision can be made with a threshold cost
Why. The expectation of a straight line is the line at the mean; for a threshold, the probability of crossing it comes from the posterior's tail.

Answer: B. The regulator's fine is invented for the question; the capstone briefs include costs of this shape (a waiting-time target, for example).

This is a knapsack problem with two nested items per district. Ranking whole actions by their own ratio, rather than incremental steps, gives a plan worth about half as much (SAR 9 million of saving against 18): the incremental steps matter. What more data is worth

  • Compute the expected value of perfect information for a decision
  • Compute the expected value of a survey by preposterior simulation

03
What more data is worth

Information is worth what it would change in the decision

Perfect informationDecide in each draw knowing the true rate; average; subtract today's best
→
The real surveySimulate its result (15% error), update, decide, average
→
Against its priceBuy where the value exceeds SAR 45,000 per district
No change, no value. A district whose best action holds across its whole posterior gains nothing from a survey.

EVPI is an upper bound; EVSI, by preposterior analysis, values the actual survey. The update in Lab 4.3 reweights the posterior draws by the survey's likelihood, which needs no new fit.

Source: Raiffa and Schlaifer (1961), Applied Statistical Decision Theory, for the preposterior analysis.

Reviewing a Bayesian analysis

  • Review an analysis against a checklist of priors, computation, checking and reporting

04
Reviewing a Bayesian analysis

Six questions, in the order of the workflow

Priors

Stated, justified, checked by prior predictive simulation?

Computation

Four chains, R-hat under 1.01, ESS, divergences explained?

Structure

Pooling suited to unequal groups, and defended?

Measurement

Is what was recorded what happened?

Checking

Predictive checks and a comparison of models?

Decision

Costs, uncertainty, remaining risk, value of data?

Each card is a day of the course. A review that finds a flaw quotes the report and says what it does to the decision; "the priors could be better" is not a finding.

Source: Gelman et al. (2020), Bayesian Workflow; the WAMBS checklist of Depaoli and van de Schoot (2017) for priors and computation.

Lab 4.4, task 3

Rank the flaws by what they do to the decision, not by how wrong they look

  1. Structure. No pooling: small districts top the list on noise; 13 zero-burst districts are declared safe.
  2. Measurement. Unrecorded bursts read as low risk in 76 districts.
  3. Decision. A ranking of means taken as the plan: no costs, intervals, remaining risk or survey price.
  4. Computation. Two chains, R-hat against 1.1, 37 divergences dismissed.
  5. Priors and checking: Normal(0, 100) called uninformative; R² as fit.

Computation looks worst to a statistician and changes the list least here: a correct fit of a no-pooling model still ranks D073 high. Other rankings pass if argued by effect on the decision. LO6 asks for at least four of the six found.
